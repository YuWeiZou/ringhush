# Dark Forest · Game Overview

> English | [中文](./游戏介绍.md)

Version: 2.5.1a  
Type: 2–8 player, simultaneous-action, hidden-information ring board game  
Rulebook: v1.0.0

## I. Basic Rules

### Map

A ring with the two ends joined, made up of a number of segments. Each segment may be:

* **Plain**: ordinary ground;
* **Pillar**: cover;
* **Ruins**: an area after bombardment.

The number of map segments, the number of Pillars, and the distribution of Ruins are public information.  
A player's own position, observation results, and received clues are private.

### Each Round

All surviving players secretly choose one action at the same time:

* Action: **Hide**; effect: stay where you are. Effective on a Pillar; on a Plain it is just idling.
* Action: **Observe**; effect: shine from yourself along one direction for one segment or the whole ring, to check whether anyone is there.
* Action: **Attack**; effect: designate a target segment and direction, resolved by the attack arc.
* Action: **Slip**; effect: move 1 segment; on a Plain you can move 2 segments.

Resolution order:

&#x20;   Hide → Observe → Slip → Attack → Death



Key timing points:

* **Observation** uses positions at the start of the round;
* **Movement** takes effect after observation;
* **Attack** uses post-movement positions;
* **Slip kills** happen before attacks;
* An attacker killed by a Slip has their attack cancelled this round and does not produce the attack's half-plane exposure.

### Information

* Observation only shows "someone/no one", not who;
* A hidden player on a Pillar is not found by observation;
* Hide on a Plain does not hide you, does not block observation, and does not block shots;
* An attack does not publicly reveal the exact segment; it gives every other player one left/right half-plane clue **relative to themselves**;
* The attacker gets no immediate feedback about "hit/empty shot/blocked/suppressed";
* There is no chat, voice, quick chat, or marking during a match.

### Bombardment

* Round 2 gives the first warning, round 4 the first explosion;
* Afterwards the warning interval and the number of targets change with the survivor count;
* Bombardment targets are public;
* Players on the target segment die, regardless of Hide/Observe/Attack/Slip and regardless of Pillar/Plain;
* After the explosion it becomes Ruins, which do not block observation or attack but do change movement topology.

### Victory

The last survivor wins.  
If all players die in the same round, it is a draw.

## II. Basic Tips

### 1\. First check whether "Hide" is useful

Hide only provides cover **on a Pillar**.  
Hide on a Plain does not hide you, does not block observation, and does not block attacks; it is usually just idling or a timeout fallback.

### 2\. Observe with a purpose

Observation is not "the farther the better".  
A long arc covers more segments, but the observation arc is also the exposure arc; the longer the arc, the more easily an attacker can bring you into their firing line.

### 3\. Observation should prioritize covering "approach paths"

A Slip kill check looks at whether the mover's **segment before moving** is inside the observation arc.  
So the observation direction should prioritize covering adjacent segments where someone might start, not just the area around yourself.

### 4\. Judge the target's state before attacking

Target hiding on a Pillar → the attack has no effect.  
Target is observing/attacking and its exposure arc contains the attacker → the attack has no effect.  
An attack is not "click the segment and it must hit"; you first have to judge what the target is probably doing this round.

### 5\. Slip is not only for escaping

Slip can:

* leave a dangerous segment;
* cross Ruins to change position;
* kill a hidden player;
* kill someone who is attacking, cancelling their attack;
* kill an observer who did not see the approach path.

But Slip is also counter-killed by an observer who "observed the approach path".

### 6\. Remember that bombardment is a public clock

Bombardment warnings are public, so they do not just kill people on the target segment—they change everyone's movement.  
If you are standing on the target segment, move first; if you are next to it, consider connectivity after the Ruins form.

## III. Advanced Techniques

This section only collects confirmed rules facts and design statements; it does not claim the game is strict rock-paper-scissors.

### 1\. The counter relationships are a conditional table, not a strict four-way cycle

What actually applies are checks with spatial conditions:

* **Hide on a Pillar**: immune to observation and attack; but killed by a single Slip, and killed by bombardment;
* **Observe**: counter-kills a Slip only when the observation arc covers the slipper's **segment before moving**;
* **Attack**: hits only when the target is observing/attacking and its exposure arc does not contain the attacker; if the attacker is killed by a Slip, the attack is cancelled;
* **Slip**: beats Hide and Attack, but is counter-killed by an Observe that saw the approach path.

So you cannot simply memorize "Hide > Observe > Slip > Attack".  
You have to look at terrain, direction, and the combination of actions this round.

### 2\. Hide on a Pillar is strong, but not invincible

Hide on a Pillar can dodge observation and ranged attacks; the costs are:

* dying when a single Slip enters the same segment;
* dying when bombardment names the segment;
* when 2 or more slippers enter the same segment at once, resolution follows the "multiple people in one segment" movement-distance rule: the hidden player who did not move has distance 0, the shortest, and survives—the slippers die.

Multiple people in one segment only compares movement distance. There are no factions, no coordinated actions, and no mechanic for several players to gang up on one target; you cannot expect "more people is safer".

### 3\. A single slipper and multiple people in one segment give different results

Current multi-player-same-segment rules compare **movement distance**, with 0 for not moving.

* 1 slipper + 1 hidden player: the hidden player dies;
* 2 or more slippers + 1 hidden player who stayed put: the hidden player's distance is 0, the shortest, so the hidden player lives and all slippers die;
* 2 or more slippers entering a player who stayed put and is attacking: the attacker's distance is 0, so the attacker lives and the slippers die.

When several people enter the same segment at once, the result is decided by movement distance.  
Before acting, confirm: is the target staying put, and will the landing spot trigger multiple-people-in-one-segment?

### 4\. The observation arc and the exposure arc are the same thing

* **The Observe arc = flashlight**: the longer it shines, the more information;
* **The Observe arc = exposure arc**: if an attacker is inside your arc, you are more likely to eat a firing line.

This is not "the stronger the observation, the better".  
Observation length trades **information** for **exposure**.

### 5\. Attack arc length is only compared in Pillar-to-Pillar mutual attacks

* Both sides on Pillars in a mutual attack: the side with the longer arc takes no damage from the shorter one; if equal, both take damage;
* One Pillar and one Plain, or two Plains, in a mutual attack: normal damage check;
* An attack from a Plain exposes the whole ring and does not enjoy "long-arc protection".

So the "Plain" role is a glass cannon:

* observing the whole ring and moving two segments gives strong information and mobility;
* once you fire, the whole ring can be the attacker;
* no Pillar cover protection.

### 6\. Two-step Slip on a Plain has a cost

On a Plain you can move 2 segments, but:

* only the final landing spot is checked; segments passed through trigger nothing;
* crossing Ruins counts as a 2-segment move;
* with multiple people in one segment, the one with the longer movement distance is more likely to be eliminated.

A two-step Slip is good for quickly opening up distance, not for blindly diving into a landing spot that may have people or observation.

### 7\. Cross-check half-plane clues; do not decide on one

The left/right clue an attack gives each other player is **relative to each of them**:

* different players may receive different left/right;
* only the side is given, not the distance;
* several clues can be crossed to narrow the range;
* the attacker also knows they were exposed, but not exactly what others received.

A single clue can only rule out half the directions; it cannot pin down a position.

### 8\. Fire results are not fed back; a hit is an assumption

The attacker only knows:

* the target segment they fired at;
* their own attack arc length.

They do not know whether the shot was empty, blocked, a hit, or suppressed by a mutual attack.  
So "I hit" is not an immediate confirmation but an assumption that has to be verified later through deaths, corpse discoveries, and changes in observation.

### 9\. Ruins are "wall + door", not a stronghold

Ruins:

* cannot be entered, cannot be occupied, cannot be crossed normally;
* **do not block observation or attack**;
* only a player standing on an adjacent Plain can cut straight through during Slip;
* Plain on one side, Pillar on the other: only Plain → Pillar;
* Plains on both sides: both directions;
* Pillars on both sides: a hard wall;
* consecutive Ruins: cannot be crossed.

So Ruins cut the ring map and also create one-way ambush spots:

* a player on the Plain side can cut across to strike once, then leave from the other side;
* a player on the Pillar side cannot cut back the same way;
* whoever crosses has to bear the risk of "not being able to come back".

### 10\. Bombardment does not just kill; it changes movement

Once a bombardment warning is public, everyone knows which segment will disappear.  
This has two effects:

* people on the target segment must move;
* others can predict "where they will move" and observe or attack in advance.

A bombardment targets an entire segment indiscriminately, not distinguishing Pillar/Plain, and not caring whether anyone is on it.  
So it is more about **shrinking the map** and **changing topology** than about punishing campers.

### 11\. 1v1 and multiplayer have different priorities

* **1v1**: little information, so position and action prediction carry more weight; long-arc observation significantly raises the risk of being attacked; bombardment forces both sides to move;
* **3+ players**: no alliances and no chat, but a de facto third-party benefit exists; whoever fires first may let a third party profit; you need to judge who the current biggest threat is, and whether you will be focused.

In multiplayer, "kill one person" is not necessarily the right goal.  
Letting the other two wear each other down is often safer than competing for the kill.

### 12\. Do not play fixed routines with an "optimal solution" mindset

The value of an action in this game depends on:

* whether your segment is a Pillar or a Plain;
* what your opponents were probably doing last round;
* where your observation arc covers;
* what your attack arc exposes;
* which paths bombardment and Ruins have changed.

The same action has completely different value under different terrain, player counts, and endgames.  
Fixed routines get read; conditional judgment is the core.

## IV. Brief Design Inspiration

What *Dark Forest* aims to be is not a "shooter", but:

**How to trade limited actions for room to survive when information is incomplete.**

The core design comes from four things:

1. **Circular map**: there is no absolutely safe corner;
2. **Simultaneous actions**: you cannot wait for others to move first; reading minds matters more than hand speed;
3. **Hidden information**: positions, observation results, and gunshots are only partially revealed;
4. **No chat**: you cannot ally by talking, only judge intent from behavior and half-plane clues.

Visually it uses a dark forest, amber actions, cold blue information, and red danger,  
making "light" itself part of the information.

## V. AI Friendliness and Interfaces

### Server

* Node.js HTTP + WebSocket;
* server-authoritative; clients only submit actions;
* each player receives an independent `view`;
* other players' positions are not sent to the current player.

### Action Interface

&#x20;   { "type": "action", "action": { "type": "attack", "target": 4, "dir": 1 } }



### AI Model Interface

Built-in models:

&#x20;   ai/models/\*.js



External JSON models:

&#x20;   ai/external/{id}/model.json



A model only gets its own view:

* its own position;
* its own observation red dots;
* gunshot left/right half-planes;
* public events;
* its own legal actions.

A model cannot get other players' true positions.

### Local Bridge

* Control port: `127.0.0.1:3210`
* Model ports: `3211–3218`
* forwards only that player's own view;
* supports manual confirmation and auto-submit;
* auto-submit keeps a 1.5-second human override window.

### Why It Is AI-Friendly

* the state is structured JSON;
* the action space is discrete;
* the rules are deterministic;
* seeded randomness makes it reproducible;
* self-play simulation is available;
* built-in baselines and an external model interface;
* clear information isolation, suitable for imperfect-information game research.

## VII. How the Tips Correspond to the AI

This only lists behaviors the simulation data can support; it does not speculate about the AI's "thoughts".

* Tip: Hide on a Pillar; what the data shows: rule-based models do use Hide; the exact frequency varies by model and player count
* Tip: Observation; what the data shows: observation rates differ clearly between models; basic mostly only observes, with 0 attacks
* Tip: long-arc attacks; what the data shows: hunter has a higher attackLongRate; survivor is lower
* Tip: Slip kills; what the data shows: survivor has a low observation ratio, moves a lot, and has the highest seat win rate in 8-player games
* Tip: half-plane clues; what the data shows: AI and humans use the same view and cannot obtain extra positions
* Tip: bombardment avoidance; what the data shows: the AI handles bombardment targets and reachability, but the simulation data cannot prove long-term planning strength
* Tip: endgames; what the data shows: adaptive is strongest in 2-player games; hunter stands out in 4-player games and survivor in 8-player games
* Tip: information hiding; what the data shows: rule-based AI has no dedicated deception module; its behavior patterns can still be read by humans

## VIII. Future Exploration Directions

### 1\. Neural networks

**Not attempted yet.**  
There is currently not enough clean state-action data with complete hidden information.

The existing 92,521 decisions can serve as a starting point for behavior cloning, but:

* 67.6% action accuracy does not equal win rate;
* the data comes from rule-based AI and has distribution bias;
* the position must be reconstructed per view, rather than feeding god view directly.

### 2\. Behavior cloning + self-play fine-tuning

&#x20;   rule-based AI matches
→ collect "view + legal actions + actual choice"
→ behavior cloning
→ self-play PPO / AlphaZero-style fine-tuning
→ evaluation in mixed matches against rule-based AI



Risks:

* the ceiling of the rule-based AI limits the student;
* imperfect information must be handled;
* self-play easily overfits to a fixed opponent.

### 3\. Opponent modeling

Player behavior has clear styles:

* conservative observation;
* aggressive attacking;
* Slip kills;
* Pillar hiding.

You can infer opponent types and dynamically adjust attack arcs and observation directions.

### 4\. Belief states

AI should not act only on "what I saw"; it should also maintain:

* where opponents might be;
* what opponents might already know;
* where opponents might move next.

This is more worthwhile than simply scaling model parameters.

### 5\. Explainability

Current AI decisions are mainly based on rules and weights.  
In the future it could output:

* why this action was chosen;
* what this observation covered;
* what this attack arc is betting on.

This helps both debugging and human learning.

### 6\. Human data

What is really missing is **human match data**.  
Rule-based AI can provide a baseline, but to make a "human-like" opponent you need human choices under the same information.

## IX. One-Sentence Summary

This is not a game about who shoots faster.  
It compares: when information is incomplete, who dares to move, who exposes themselves first, and who can hide at the right moment.
